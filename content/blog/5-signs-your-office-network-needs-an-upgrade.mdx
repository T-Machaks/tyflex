Office networks tend to grow the way most infrastructure does — one switch, one access point, one extra device at a time — until one day nobody can quite explain why the Wi-Fi in the boardroom is unusable. Here are the signs worth acting on before they become a full outage.

## 1. Wi-Fi that's fine near the router and unusable everywhere else

This almost always means access point coverage hasn't kept pace with how the space is actually used. A single access point covers a fixed radius — added partitions, extra floors, or a growing headcount all shrink effective coverage without anyone changing the hardware.

## 2. Switches running hot, or unexplained port failures

Unmanaged switches purchased years ago for a much smaller office are a common bottleneck. If ports are failing, or a switch feels hot to the touch, it's usually past the point of quietly limping along.

## 3. No visibility into what's actually happening on the network

If a slowdown means someone unplugging things one at a time to find the cause, there's no monitoring in place. A managed network gives you a dashboard — which device, which port, which access point is causing the problem — instead of a guessing game.

## 4. New hires waiting days for network access

This is usually a structured cabling and switch capacity problem rather than a software one. If adding a desk means running new cable through the ceiling, growth is going to keep being painful.

## 5. Guest and staff traffic sharing the same network

This is a security gap as much as a performance one. Without VLAN segmentation, a compromised guest device sits on the same network as your servers and POS terminals.

## What an upgrade actually involves

It's rarely a full rip-and-replace. Most upgrades we scope involve a site survey to map real coverage gaps, replacing aging unmanaged switches with PoE-capable managed ones, and segmenting traffic with VLANs — usually phased over a few weeks rather than a single disruptive weekend.

If any of the five signs above sound familiar, a network audit is a low-cost way to find out exactly what's worth fixing first.
